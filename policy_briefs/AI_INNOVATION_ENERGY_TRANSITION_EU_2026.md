# Policy Brief: Closing Europe’s AI Diffusion Gap While Accelerating the Green Transition

**Portfolio policy brief · September 2026**  
**Author: Stanley Osei-Wusu**

## Executive summary

European businesses are adopting artificial intelligence rapidly, but adoption remains uneven by firm size and country. At the same time, the energy transition continues to advance but remains short of the European Union’s 2030 renewable-energy target, while recent R&D growth in the EU has been modest.

The policy challenge is therefore not simply to produce more frontier technology. It is to **diffuse useful technology more broadly while maintaining the innovation, skills and clean-energy capacity needed to support it**.

Three current indicators illustrate the problem:

- In 2025, **20.0% of EU enterprises with at least 10 employees used AI technologies**, up from 13.5% in 2024.
- Adoption remained strongly size-dependent: approximately **17% of small enterprises, 30.4% of medium enterprises and 55.0% of large enterprises** used AI in 2025.
- Provisional Eurostat data indicate that renewables accounted for **26.2% of EU gross final energy consumption in 2025**, compared with a 2030 target of 42.5%.
- OECD estimates indicate that inflation-adjusted EU R&D expenditure grew by only **0.4% in 2024**.

These figures do not establish a causal relationship between AI adoption, R&D and renewable-energy performance. They do show why digital, innovation and energy policy should not be analysed in isolation.

## 1. AI adoption is rising, but diffusion is unequal

Eurostat reports that 20.0% of EU enterprises used at least one AI technology in 2025, a 6.5 percentage-point increase from 2024.

Among the ten countries used in this repository’s comparative analytical framework, the 2025 enterprise-AI figures show substantial variation:

| Country | Enterprises using AI, 2025 |
| --- | ---: |
| Denmark | 42.0% |
| Finland | 37.8% |
| Sweden | 35.0% |
| Netherlands | ~33%* |
| Austria | ~30% |
| Germany | ~26% |
| Spain | ~20% |
| France | ~18% |
| Italy | ~16.5% |
| Poland | 8.4% |

*Eurostat marks a break in the Netherlands time series. Rounded values are used where the published figure is presented graphically.

The size gap is equally important. Eurostat reports AI use in 2025 at 55.0% among large enterprises, 30.4% among medium enterprises and 17.0% among small enterprises. The 2026 Eurostat digitalisation publication summarises the combined SME figure at about 19%.

### Policy implication

A policy focused only on national AI champions or large enterprises risks confusing **frontier capability with broad productive diffusion**.

For inclusive digital transformation, policy monitoring should therefore ask not only:

> How much AI is being adopted?

but also:

> Which firms, sectors, regions and workers are able to adopt it — and which are not?

This is particularly relevant to SME productivity, workforce transition and regional inequality.

## 2. Digital transformation is occurring alongside an unfinished energy transition

Eurostat’s provisional 2025 data indicate that renewable sources supplied 26.2% of EU gross final energy consumption, up from 25.2% in 2024. The EU’s 2030 target is 42.5%.

Eurostat estimates that reaching that target would require an average annual increase of approximately 3.3 percentage points from 2026 through 2030.

The leading countries in the provisional 2025 renewable-energy figures were:

- Sweden — 65.4%;
- Finland — 53.0%;
- Denmark — 48.2%.

These same three countries were also among the EU leaders in enterprise AI adoption in 2025.

That overlap is analytically interesting, but it should **not** be interpreted as evidence that renewable-energy performance causes AI adoption, or vice versa. Institutional quality, industrial structure, income, digital skills, firm composition, energy resources and public policy may all contribute.

### Policy implication

Digital and green-transition indicators should increasingly be analysed together, while preserving causal caution. Rapid expansion of data centres, AI workloads and digital infrastructure makes the interaction between digital capacity, electricity systems and clean-energy investment increasingly relevant to long-term economic planning.

## 3. Innovation capacity cannot be assumed

OECD Main Science and Technology Indicators show that R&D investment continued to grow across the OECD in 2024, but EU inflation-adjusted R&D expenditure grew by only 0.4% that year.

OECD data place EU27 gross domestic expenditure on R&D at about 2.13% of GDP in 2024, while Germany was around 3.13%.

R&D intensity is not a complete measure of innovation capacity. It does, however, provide useful context when interpreting technology adoption. A country can be strong in research but slower in diffusion, or strong in technology use without leading in domestic R&D.

### Policy implication

Innovation policy should distinguish at least three separate questions:

1. **Creation** — is an economy generating or funding research and innovation?
2. **Diffusion** — are technologies reaching SMEs, regions and non-frontier sectors?
3. **Complementary capacity** — are skills, infrastructure, finance and energy systems able to support adoption?

Combining these dimensions produces a more useful policy picture than any single technology-adoption ranking.

## 4. Four priorities for inclusive digital and green transformation

### 1. Measure diffusion, not only national averages

Firm-size, sectoral and regional breakdowns should accompany headline AI-adoption statistics. A national average can conceal a large divide between leading enterprises and smaller firms.

### 2. Treat digital skills as infrastructure

Eurostat reports that 40% of EU citizens lacked basic or above-basic digital skills in 2025. Technology investment without corresponding human capability can widen rather than close adoption gaps.

### 3. Link AI strategy to energy and infrastructure planning

Digitalisation and decarbonisation should not be treated as independent portfolios. Energy availability, grid carbon intensity, infrastructure investment and technology growth increasingly interact.

### 4. Build policy dashboards that expose uncertainty

International datasets have different reporting calendars, definitions, missingness patterns and revision schedules. Public dashboards should make those differences visible rather than silently filling gaps or presenting non-comparable years as equivalent.

## Conclusion

Europe’s current challenge is not simply an AI race. It is a **diffusion, capability and transition problem**.

AI adoption is accelerating, yet the gap between large and small enterprises remains substantial. Renewable energy is expanding, yet the EU remains well short of its 2030 target. R&D capacity remains essential, but investment growth alone does not guarantee broad technology diffusion.

A more inclusive policy approach would therefore connect **technology adoption, SME capability, digital skills, innovation investment, energy transition and data quality** within a common evidence framework.

The practical lesson for policymakers is straightforward:

> Do not measure technological progress only by what the leading firms can do. Measure whether productive capability is spreading across the wider economy — and whether the infrastructure, skills and energy system can sustain it.
